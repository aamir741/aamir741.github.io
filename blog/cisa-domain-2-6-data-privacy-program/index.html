CISA Domain 2.6: Data Privacy Program and Principles

CISA Domain 2.6: privacy versus security, data subject, controller and processor, the seven privacy principles, privacy documentation, frameworks and the privacy audit process.

Muhammad Aamir · 22 November 2026

A customer app encrypts every location record it collects, but its privacy notice mentions only delivery details. Is the encryption enough? On the CISA exam, no. This lesson covers CISA Domain 2, Section 2.6: how a data privacy program governs the collection, use, sharing and retention of personal information.

2.6 What privacy covers

Privacy concerns how personal information is collected, used, shared, retained and made available to individuals. Security helps protect that information, but secure processing can still be unfair, excessive or outside an authorized purpose. A privacy program addresses those decisions across the information life cycle.

  • A data subject is an identifiable person to whom personal information relates.
  • A controller determines the purposes and means of processing.
  • A processor processes information on the controller's behalf.

These roles depend on actual decisions and activities, not merely the titles written in a contract.

A privacy program needs sponsorship, accountable ownership, data visibility, applicable requirements, policies, training, individual-rights processes, supplier controls, incident arrangements and ongoing review. A data protection officer or privacy officer advises and monitors within the applicable mandate; appointing that person does not transfer all enterprise accountability to them.

The seven privacy principles

Principle Practical meaning
Lawfulness, fairness and transparency Use an appropriate basis and explain the processing clearly
Purpose limitation Use information for defined purposes and assess proposed changes
Data minimization Collect and use only what the purpose requires
Accuracy Maintain appropriate accuracy and correct errors across systems
Storage limitation Keep identifiable information only as justified, under approved retention rules
Security Protect against inappropriate access, alteration and loss
Accountability Be able to demonstrate that the required practices are implemented

These principles use GDPR-style terminology. The applicable jurisdiction determines the precise duties, exceptions and time limits, so identify the actual requirement before treating a right, consent condition or notification deadline as universal.

Protected health information (PHI) is individually identifiable health information within the scope of the US HIPAA framework. It can be electronic, paper or oral. A patient record held by a covered entity can qualify; not every health-related datum worldwide does.

2.6.1 Privacy documentation

Privacy documentation records both what the enterprise tells people and how it manages the processing internally. A clear notice supports transparency; an internal processing inventory supports control. Neither replaces the other, and a signed form does not make unnecessary or unlawful processing acceptable.

Record What an auditor checks
Privacy notice Clear, accessible and consistent with actual processing
Consent record Scope, time, affirmative action, notice version and withdrawal handling
Personal information inventory Coverage of suppliers, copies, exports and unintentional collection
Privacy impact assessment (PIA) Decisions, risks, owners and remediation are acted on
Data protection impact assessment (DPIA) Applicability and follow-up match the governing requirements
Processing agreements Subprocessing, incidents, transfers, assurance and exit obligations
Rights, incident and training records Identity checks, timeliness, decisions, escalation and completed actions
Privacy governance reports Reliable measures, and significant concerns reach decision-makers

Example: a customer application collects location data although its notice describes only order delivery details. Encryption protects the data in storage, but it does not explain or authorize the extra collection. Review necessity, purpose, lawful basis and transparency before treating the security controls as a complete answer.

Privacy frameworks

Reference Purpose
OECD Privacy Principles Eight principles, from collection limitation to accountability
AICPA Generally Accepted Privacy Principles Privacy management and assessment across the information life cycle
ISACA Privacy Principles Organizing privacy responsibilities, practices and program evaluation
ISO/TS 17975 Health-informatics guidance on consent for personal health information
NIST Privacy Framework A voluntary approach to privacy risk management, separate from the NIST Cybersecurity Framework

Select the applicable legal requirements first, then use a suitable framework to make the controls complete and consistent. NIST Privacy Framework 1.0 organizes outcomes into Identify-P, Govern-P, Control-P, Communicate-P and Protect-P; check the version before treating a draft or later document as an established requirement.

2.6.2 Audit process

  1. Understand the program's scope, applicable obligations, processing activities and responsible people.
  2. Assess the inventory and data flows before selecting samples.
  3. Trace representative information from collection through use, supplier handling, retention and disposal, including new services and processing outside the core IT inventory.
  4. Test whether notices reflect reality, processing has a suitable basis, access and sharing are controlled, rights requests are handled, and retention and incident processes operate. Examine both design and operation.

Audit focus: combine processing records, contracts, impact assessments, configuration inspection, sample requests, deletion evidence and interviews. Avoid collecting unnecessary live personal information as audit evidence. A privacy finding should describe the affected individuals, processing and consequences as well as the missing control.

Example: customer support recordings are proposed for model training. Review whether the new use is permitted, which people and data are affected, the provider's terms, access, retention, transfers and deletion. Removing names alone may leave identifiable content, and pseudonymized data can remain personal information when linkage is possible.

Auditor perspective: trace a personal information request

A customer asks for access to personal information. The service desk can find the account profile, but relevant recordings are held by a provider. Trace the request through identity verification, search, authorized release, documented exclusions and provider assistance. Check how the applicable deadlines are established, test that the response contains only the requesting person's information, and confirm unnecessary copies are removed under the approved retention arrangements. Encrypting the response supports confidentiality; scope, purpose and authorized release are separate privacy questions.

Practice questions

1. An app collects customer location data that its privacy notice does not mention, although the data are encrypted. What should the IS auditor do FIRST? Answer: determine whether the collection is necessary and lawful. Encryption protects the data but does not authorize collecting it.

2. An enterprise appoints a data protection officer. Who remains PRIMARILY accountable for the enterprise's privacy compliance? Answer: enterprise management that owns the processing. The officer advises and monitors; the appointment does not transfer accountability.
