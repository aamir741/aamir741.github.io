CISA Domain 4.13: System and Operational Resilience

CISA Domain 4.13: high availability, failover, replication, network diversity, shared failure domains, MAO and the service delivery objective.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A company buys a second internet carrier for its data center. Both fibers enter the building through the same underground duct, and the second carrier's name on the invoice feels reassuring. One excavator later, both are gone. Is that resilience? This lesson covers CISA Domain four, section 4.13: system and operational resilience.

4.13 Resilience basics

Resilience combines the ability to withstand disruption, adapt and recover. It is about keeping required outcomes going, not about owning a lot of spare equipment. The exam keeps asking whether the spare equipment would really have helped in the scenario given.

High availability minimizes service interruption. Fault tolerance maintains required operation through specified failures. And disaster recovery restores capability after a disruptive event.

  • High availability: Minimizes service interruption
  • Fault tolerance: Keeps operating through specified failures
  • Disaster recovery: Restores capability afterwards

Exam Trap: Here's the catch. All three are bounded by the assumptions they were designed for. Redundancy against one disk failure does not establish resilience against fire or ransomware.

Different Timescales: High availability, fault tolerance and disaster recovery address related but different mechanisms and timescales. Load balancing doesn't automatically provide independent failover, and failover itself can introduce data consistency or capacity risks. So never treat one feature as proof of the others.

4.13.1 Application resiliency

A cluster coordinates multiple nodes. Designs can be active passive or active active. Load balancing distributes traffic, but needs healthy back-end capacity and protection of its own failure dependencies. Failover switches service to an alternate component or location, and failback returns it under control.

  • Cluster: Coordinates multiple nodes
  • Active/passive or active/active: Two common designs
  • Load balancing: Spreads traffic
  • Failover and failback: Switch over, then return

Split-Brain: Then examine state, sessions, transaction consistency and split brain risk. That's where isolated nodes can both act as active owners. Quorum or fencing mechanisms can reduce that risk. If both halves of a cluster keep writing, you can end up with two conflicting versions of the same data, and that is worse than a short outage.

Don't only test a clean planned switchover. Test behavior under network partition, storage failure and dependency outage. Real failures are messy, and a design that only works in a tidy rehearsal has not been proven.

  • Network partition: Nodes can't see each other
  • Storage failure: Data layer fails
  • Dependency outage: Something upstream is gone

Synchronous replication waits for the required remote acknowledgment before completing a write. It can reduce data loss exposure, but adds latency and depends on distance, capacity and connectivity. Asynchronous replication has less performance impact, but permits lag and potential data loss.

Replication Trap: Neither method automatically protects against replicated deletion or corruption. A copy of a mistake is still a mistake. Replication keeps systems in step, so it will faithfully carry a bad change to the second site.

4.13.2 Network resiliency

Identify single points of failure across routers, switches, firewalls, carriers, local loops, building entrances, power, DNS and management dependencies.

  • Routers and firewalls: Switches too
  • Carriers and local loops: Plus building entrances
  • Power: And DNS
  • Management paths: Often forgotten

Two carriers, one duct: Two suppliers or circuits may share a physical conduit, exchange, upstream provider or power source. Two internet circuits from different carriers can enter through the same duct, so one excavation removes both. Ask for evidence of independence, rather than accepting the supplier names on the diagram.

Alternative routing provides another route when the primary path is unavailable. Diverse routing emphasizes physical path separation. Long haul diversity means independence beyond local access, since two brand names can share infrastructure. And last mile protection covers local loops, building entrances and power. Terminology varies by provider, so inspect the actual routes.

  • Alternative routing: Another route when primary fails
  • Diverse routing: Physical path separation
  • Long-haul diversity: Independent carrier backbone
  • Last-mile protection: Local access and building entry

A ping is not proof: For converged voice and data, verify bandwidth, latency, jitter, packet loss and quality of service. Test that backup links can carry the required recovery workload. A link that permits a successful ping may still be too small. Cellular backup can help, but shared regional failures or congestion can still affect it. Last mile resilience needs particular attention, meaning separate local access paths and separate building entry.

Shared failure domains

Now failure domains: groups exposed to a single cause of failure. Think of them as the things that fail together, even when the equipment looks separate.

Common Causes: Redundancy can reduce a component failure, but leave common causes untouched. Separate servers are not independent if they sit on the same foundations. Assess concentration and correlated events across regions, facilities, providers and workforce. Ask one question of every pair of copies: what single cause could take out both?

Separate servers may share power, network, storage, identity, administrative control, or a vulnerable software version.

  • Power: Same feed
  • Network: Same fabric
  • Storage: Same array
  • Identity: Same directory
  • Administrators: Same control
  • Software version: Same flaw

Logical Failure: Say two cloud regions share one compromised administrator, and replication propagates encrypted data. Regional separation doesn't address that logical failure.

Consider isolated recovery copies, protected credentials, a clean rebuild, trusted configuration and a controlled return to service. Recovery from a logical failure is a rebuild, not a simple switch to the other region.

  • Isolated copies: Recovery copies kept apart
  • Protected credentials: Not shared with attackers
  • Clean rebuild: From trusted configuration
  • Controlled return: Back to service

Degraded Modes: Test degraded modes and manual workarounds at realistic volumes, including how records are later reconciled. A workaround that functions for ten transactions may fail during a week-long outage.

Validate with evidence: Validate resilience through scenarios and evidence, with appropriate operational authorization and safeguards. The auditor reviews exercise results and the approvals behind them, rather than breaking a live service to see what happens.

Outage tolerance

Last, outage tolerance and alternate service capability.

The maximum allowable outage, or M A O, is the interruption limit before business consequences become unacceptable. Some sources call it the maximum acceptable outage. Then M T D and M T O may express the same tolerance, but establish the boundary rather than assuming the labels are interchangeable. Unacceptable consequences need not mean irreparable damage. Section 4.12 explains how technical recovery, work recovery and business tolerance fit together.

  • MAO: Limit before consequences are unacceptable
  • Other labels: Also maximum acceptable outage
  • MTD and MTO: May mean the same boundary

Sdo is not a time: The service delivery objective, or S D O, is the required service capability during recovery, such as an approved transaction throughput. It is not another time interval, so a recovery can meet every deadline and still miss the objective.

Assess resilience against time, data currency and usable service capacity together.

  • Time: Back inside the limit
  • Data currency: How fresh the data is
  • Usable capacity: Enough business work

Fast but too small: A mirrored system that starts promptly, but supports too little business work, can still fail the approved recovery objectives.

Auditor perspective

Now the auditor perspective.

Real-World Example: Primary and alternate systems sit in separate locations, but both need the same certificate service and privileged cloud identity. Physical separation reduces some disruption scenarios. The service stays exposed to dependencies both environments share. If that identity is unavailable, neither site can start.

Map failure domains across facilities, networks, data, credentials and control planes. Ask which independent authority can recover the service if the shared identity is unavailable. And examine alternate access arrangements and evidence from a representative exercise.

  • Map failure domains: Facilities, networks, data, credentials
  • Independent recovery: If the shared identity is lost
  • Evidence: From a representative exercise

Practice questions

Test yourself with three exam-style questions. To recap. Redundancy against one failure isn't resilience against every failure. Trace the actual paths to prove independence. Look for shared failure domains like identity, power and administrators. And judge recovery on time, data currency and capacity, because the S D O is not a time. When an exam scenario shows two of anything, ask what they share before you call it resilient.

  • Bounded by design: One failure isn't all failures
  • Prove independence: Trace actual paths
  • Shared failure domains: Identity, power, admin
  • Time, data, capacity: SDO isn't a time

Practice questions

1. A company has internet circuits from two different carriers. What should the IS auditor do FIRST to assess network resilience? Answer: Trace the physical paths for shared conduits or entrances. Option A, service credits, compensate after an outage but don't make the routes diverse. Option B, the load balancer, can't create independent paths. And option D, the ping, shows only that the link answers, not that it can carry the recovery workload. The first step is always to trace the real paths.

2. Primary and alternate sites need the same certificate service and privileged cloud identity. What is the PRIMARY concern? Answer: A shared dependency could disable both environments. Option B is the trap, because separate locations are a strength, not a concern. Option C, the replication method, doesn't address the common failure domain. And option D, a missing owner, is a lesser issue than a dependency that disables both sites. Always look for what the environments share.

3. A mirrored system restarts within the approved outage limit but supports only a fraction of normal transactions. Which objective is MOST likely unmet? Answer: The service delivery objective. Option A is the trap: the system restarted within the limit, so the outage objective was met. Option B, replication lag, concerns data currency, not capacity. And option C, failback, is about returning to the primary site, not service during recovery. Remember, the S D O is capability, not time.
