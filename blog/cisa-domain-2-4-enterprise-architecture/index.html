CISA Domain 2.4: Enterprise Architecture and Considerations

CISA Domain 2.4: the four architecture layers, transition roadmaps, TOGAF and Zachman, audit focus, and using EA to expose shared dependencies.

Muhammad Aamir · 20 November 2026

Two divisions each buy a customer platform, and both projects meet their requirements. So why can't the enterprise produce one reliable view of its customers? This lesson covers CISA Domain 2, Section 2.4: how enterprise architecture connects business needs with information, applications and technology, and what the IS auditor checks.

2.4.1 Architecture layers and the transition roadmap

Enterprise architecture (EA) is a coordinated description of how the enterprise works and the capabilities it needs. It describes the current and desired states and guides the transition, so the enterprise can make coherent investment and design decisions.

Layer Question it answers Retailer example
Business Which capability and process are needed? A single customer return process across stores and online sales
Data / information What information and definitions support it? Common customer, order and product identifiers
Application Which systems provide the capability and exchange information? The sales platform connects with stock, payment and returns services
Technology What infrastructure enables the systems? Network, compute, identity and integration services with agreed resilience

The layers influence one another, so a change in one usually reaches the others.

A target architecture without a transition roadmap is only an aspiration. The roadmap identifies dependencies, standards, gaps, costs, risks and intermediate states. Architecture decision and exception processes stop individual projects from creating incompatible solutions without a justified business reason.

2.4.2 TOGAF and Zachman

  • TOGAF provides an architecture approach, including the Architecture Development Method (ADM).
  • Zachman organizes architecture descriptions by perspectives and by questions: what, how, where, who, when and why.

Exam trap: these are architecture frameworks. They are not interchangeable with service management or information security management systems.

Audit focus

The IS auditor reviews:

  • approved architecture principles
  • current and target models
  • the roadmap
  • design decisions and exceptions

Then trace selected investments to the architecture, and look for five red flags: duplicated capabilities, inconsistent data, unsupported interfaces, technology obsolescence, and concentration on a single provider.

Example: two divisions buy customer platforms with incompatible identifiers. Each project meets its local requirements, but the enterprise cannot produce a reliable consolidated customer view. Architecture governance should expose the shared data requirement before investment and assign ownership of the integration decision.

2.4.3 Using enterprise architecture to expose dependencies

The current state shows how the enterprise operates. The target state and transition roadmap describe the intended change. Assess whether architecture principles and decision processes support strategy, interoperability, resilience, security and manageable technical debt.

Useful evidence includes service and data-flow models, interface inventories, technology standards, approved deviations and transition decisions. Test a sample against reality: an attractive diagram that omits an unmanaged identity dependency may mislead project and recovery planning. Governance should allow justified exceptions while making their risk and support consequences visible.

Example: two business units propose separate customer platforms. A shared customer definition and controlled interfaces may improve consistency, while one central platform could create a concentration or migration risk.

Don't assume centralization is inherently best. Evaluate the business objective and feasible options, and trace important dependencies into the BIA, change impact analysis and supplier oversight. Architecture is valuable when its information changes decisions and is maintained after implementation.

Worked case: find the shared dependency

Three channels are scheduled to move to separate hosting environments, but each still uses one customer identity service and one data integration route. Compare the current and target architecture, data definitions, interfaces and owners. Follow a customer request through each channel to see which dependencies remain shared. Ask whether the transition plan addresses service interruption, and whether tests exercise the shared identity and data services. A hosting diagram can be accurate and still omit the dependency that determines the business outcome.

Practice questions

1. An IS auditor finds that a target enterprise architecture has been approved, but no transition roadmap exists. What is the GREATEST concern?

A. The target models may not follow the TOGAF ADM B. Dependencies, gaps and intermediate states are unplanned C. Current-state models may be outdated by year end D. The architecture team may lack modelling tools

Answer: B. Without a roadmap, the target is only an aspiration. The framework used and tooling are secondary.

2. Two business units each propose their own customer platform, and management suggests one central platform instead. What is the IS auditor's MOST appropriate position?

A. Assess the business objective and feasible options, with their risks B. Support the central platform, as centralization is inherently better C. Accept separate platforms, as each meets its own unit's needs D. Delay any decision until a full Zachman model has been built

Answer: A. Centralization can create concentration or migration risk, and local fit doesn't guarantee an enterprise outcome.
