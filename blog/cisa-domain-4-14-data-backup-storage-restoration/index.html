CISA Domain 4.14: Data Backup, Storage and Restoration

CISA Domain 4.14: RAID, snapshots, offsite and cloud backup, backup schemes, recovery points, restore testing and cyber recovery.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

The backup report shows green every single night. Then ransomware hits, and the recovery team discovers the decryption key lived inside the affected environment. Was the data really protected? This lesson covers CISA Domain four, section 4.14: data backup, storage and restoration.

Backups, storage resilience and restoration must be evaluated together.

Backups preserve recoverable data and supporting information. Storage resilience helps sustain access through certain failures. And restoration turns protected copies into usable business capability.

  • Backups: Preserve recoverable data and support information
  • Storage resilience: Sustains access through certain failures
  • Restoration: Turns copies into usable capability

Exam Trap: Backup success, media readability and functioning storage are each insufficient alone. An auditor looks at whether the business can actually get its service back.

4.14.1 Storage resiliency

RAID combines disks for performance, redundancy, or both. RAID zero stripes without redundancy. RAID one mirrors. RAID five uses distributed parity and commonly tolerates one disk failure. RAID six adds dual parity and commonly tolerates two. And RAID ten combines mirroring and striping, with failure tolerance depending on which disks fail. Rebuild performance and remaining exposure matter, because a degraded array is one more failure away from data loss.

  • RAID 0: Stripes, no redundancy
  • RAID 1: Mirrors
  • RAID 5: Distributed parity, commonly one disk
  • RAID 6: Dual parity, commonly two disks
  • RAID 10: Mirroring plus striping

Raid is not a backup: RAID is not a backup. It keeps a service running through a disk failure, but a deleted or corrupted file is deleted or corrupted on every disk.

Direct-attached storage connects directly to a host. Network-attached storage commonly provides file-level access. And a storage area network commonly provides block-level storage connectivity.

  • Direct-attached: Connects straight to a host
  • Network-attached: Commonly file-level access
  • Storage area network: Commonly block-level connectivity

Redundant Paths: Availability depends on controllers, paths, fabrics, power and configuration, as well as disks. A second path that nobody has ever tested is a hope, not a control.

4.14.2 Backup and restoration

Define scope, frequency, retention, recovery points, encryption, keys, access and test requirements from business needs. Include application-consistent data, configuration, source artifacts, certificates and recovery instructions where needed.

  • Scope and frequency: Plus retention
  • Recovery points: Driven by the business
  • Encryption and keys: Plus access
  • Test requirements: Written in advance

Incomplete Backup: A database backup without its necessary keys, logs or compatible software may be unusable. The data is only one ingredient of the recovery, so check that the whole recipe is protected.

A snapshot captures a point in time state. Some snapshots depend on the original storage or shared credentials, so they may not be independent recoverable copies. Replication provides another copy, but it may rapidly replicate deletion, corruption or encryption by malware. So assess retention and isolation, not only the number of copies.

  • Snapshot: Point-in-time state, may depend on original storage
  • Replication: Another copy, but copies deletion too
  • Assess: Retention and isolation, not just count

4.14.3 Offsite and cloud

Sections 4.14.3 and 4.14.4: offsite libraries, cloud backup, media and procedures.

Keep recoverable copies outside the relevant shared failure domains. Assess environmental and physical protection, access, transport, inventory, location tracking and retrieval time. For irreplaceable information, protect against loss in transfer with an additional suitable copy.

  • Environmental and physical protection: At the vault
  • Access and transport: Who can touch the media
  • Inventory and tracking: Where each item is
  • Retrieval time: Fast enough for recovery

Encryption Limit: Encryption addresses disclosure; it does not recreate lost media. Don't let a strong cipher stand in for a second copy. If the only tape is lost in transit, encryption keeps it private, but the data is still gone.

Cloud backup requires clarity about service scope, regions, retention, immutable settings, deletion rights, keys, egress time, bandwidth and exit.

  • Scope and regions: What is covered, where
  • Retention and immutability: Plus deletion rights
  • Keys: Who holds them
  • Egress and exit: Time, bandwidth, leaving

Shared Credentials: Shared production credentials can make both live data and cloud copies vulnerable. An offsite copy is valuable, but independent access and recoverability also matter.

Select disk, tape, cloud or other media according to capacity, durability, access, recovery time and cost. Protect recovery documentation and credentials so they remain available during an outage while access stays controlled. Retain compatible readers, software and keys for the retention period. And monitor jobs, investigate failures and perform restore tests.

  • Choose media: Capacity, durability, access, cost
  • Protect documentation: Available but controlled
  • Keep readers and keys: For the retention period
  • Monitor and restore-test: Investigate failures

New Systems: Use transaction consistent backups, or the logs and procedures needed for consistent recovery. And include new systems and data in the backup inventory as part of change and asset management, or they simply won't be protected. Many surprise gaps are systems that went live after the backup list was written.

4.14.5 Backup schemes

A full backup copies all selected data. An incremental copies changes since the previous relevant backup. A differential copies changes since the last full backup. Transaction log backup or remote journaling captures changes for recovery to a chosen point. And electronic vaulting transfers backup information electronically to a remote location.

  • Full: All selected data
  • Incremental: Changes since the previous backup
  • Differential: Changes since the last full
  • Transaction log: Recover to a chosen point
  • Electronic vaulting: Transfer to a remote location

To restore, an incremental scheme needs a suitable full backup and every required incremental, in order. A differential scheme needs the suitable full backup and only the latest required differential.

Worked Example: Sunday is a full backup, and Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday are incrementals. To recover Wednesday's state, restore Sunday plus each required incremental in sequence. With differentials, Sunday plus Wednesday's differential is typically sufficient. Missing an intermediate incremental can break the chain.

Labels Vary: Products can use synthetic fulls, incremental forever or other mechanisms with different restore behavior. So verify the tool's actual dependencies rather than relying only on its labels.

4.14.6 Rotation and recovery points

Grandfather father son is a rotation scheme using monthly, weekly and daily generations. Rotation is not the backup method. Retention should reflect recovery scenarios and obligations, including the need to find a clean point before delayed corruption is discovered. Maintain records linking media to contents, dates, protection, location, retention and disposal. And protect access to catalogs and restore functions, as well as the data.

  • Grandfather-father-son: Monthly, weekly and daily generations
  • Retention: Find a clean point before delayed corruption
  • Media records: Contents, dates, location, disposal
  • Protect catalogs: And restore functions

Rpo reality check: The recovery point objective drives the protection design, but a scheduled fifteen minute job doesn't guarantee a fifteen minute achievable recovery point. Failures, transfer delays, replication lag and missing logs can extend the loss. Imagine two hours of failed copies: the schedule says fifteen minutes, but the newest usable data is two hours old. Examine the actual latest usable consistent data, transfer delays, missing logs and protected failure domains.

Testing and cyber recovery

Now testing recoverability and cyber recovery.

Restore representative critical services into a controlled environment and verify integrity, functionality, timing and data currency. Include dependencies and the intended operating load.

  • Representative services: Restored into a controlled environment
  • Integrity and function: Does it work?
  • Timing and currency: Fast and fresh enough
  • Dependencies and load: As intended

One small file: A successful restore of one small file cannot establish whole-service recovery. The test should reconcile the restored business data too, and show who could unlock the keys.

Maintain appropriately isolated, offline or immutable copies according to the threat and design. Protect retention changes and deletion with separate authority. Cyber recovery may require a clean environment, validated identities and keys, malware assessment, clean restoration points and careful reconnection. Prevent reinfection while restoring business priorities. Restoring fast from an infected copy only brings the attack back.

  • Isolated, offline or immutable copies: Matched to the threat
  • Separate authority: For retention changes and deletion
  • Clean environment: Validated identities and keys
  • Careful reconnection: Prevent reinfection

Mirror is not a backup: Disk mirroring, with RAID one a common example, supports availability when a covered disk fails. But a mirror normally applies the same logical writes, so deletion, corruption or ransomware driven changes may affect both copies. Surviving a disk failure doesn't demonstrate recovery of yesterday's clean records after today's destructive write.

Now the auditor perspective.

Real-World Example: The backup inventory shows protected copies with retention locks. Then a restore test fails because the team can't get the decryption key from the affected environment. A retained copy addresses preservation. Successful recovery also depends on authorized access, usable keys, configuration and tested restoration procedures.

Inspect backup completeness, separate access, key recovery, and the ability to validate application data after restore. And confirm that privileged accounts cannot silently shorten the protection needed for recovery.

  • Backup completeness: Everything needed is there
  • Separate access and keys: Key recovery works
  • Data validation: After the restore
  • Privileged accounts: Can't silently shorten protection

Practice questions

Test yourself with three exam-style questions. To recap. RAID is not a backup. Keep independent, retained copies, isolated from production credentials. Make sure keys, logs and the full backup chain are present. And test restoration of whole services, not one file.

  • RAID isn't backup: Mirrors copy bad writes
  • Independent copies: Isolated and retained
  • Keys and chain: All dependencies present
  • Test whole services: Not one file

Practice questions

1. A company treats RAID 5 on its file server as its backup. What is the IS auditor's MOST significant concern? Answer: Deletion or corruption reaches every disk, leaving no clean copy. Option A, long rebuilds, is a real exposure, but it's secondary. Option C, hot swappable disks, is a convenience issue. And option D, firmware, is routine maintenance. The core gap is that RAID is not a backup, so there is no earlier clean copy.

2. Which evidence BEST demonstrates that a critical service can be recovered after an outage? Answer: A timed restore of the whole service into a controlled environment. Option A, the completion report, shows jobs ran, not that data can be restored. Option B, one sample file, can't establish whole service recovery. And option C counts copies, but numbers say nothing about usability, timing or integrity.

3. Sunday is a full backup, and Monday to Wednesday are conventional incrementals. What is needed to restore Wednesday's state? Answer: Sunday's full backup and every incremental through Wednesday, in order. Option B skips Monday and Tuesday, which breaks the chain. Option C treats an incremental as complete, but it holds only that day's changes. And option D stops at Monday, so Tuesday and Wednesday's changes are lost.
