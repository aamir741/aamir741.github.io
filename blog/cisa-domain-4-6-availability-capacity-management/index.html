CISA Domain 4.6: Systems Availability and Capacity Management

CISA Domain 4.6: availability versus capacity, architecture and operating system reviews, utilities, licensing, capacity management, availability, MTBF and MTTR, and testing capacity during failover.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A payment service has plenty of spare CPU, yet customers wait ten seconds for every transaction. Is capacity fine? Not necessarily. This lesson covers CISA Domain 4, Section 4.6: how an IS auditor judges availability, capacity and the controls around operating software.

4.6 Availability versus capacity

Availability means a service is usable when required. Capacity management ensures sufficient resources for current and expected demand at an acceptable cost. High utilization is not necessarily good performance: a saturated queue, storage device or network can make a business service unusable.

4.6.1 to 4.6.2 Architecture and operating systems

Architecture connects applications, operating systems, databases, communications, utilities and hardware. Identify shared dependencies and distinguish logical separation from physical independence. Ten virtual servers on one host do not provide ten independent failure domains.

An operating system manages processes, memory, storage and devices, and enforces isolation. Protect privileged code and configuration, use supported versions and secure baselines, and control administrative accounts, remote access and service identities.

  • Parameters affect resource allocation, priorities, authentication, auditing and data handling. Document approved settings; defaults are not evidence of suitability.
  • Hardening removes unnecessary functions while retaining required business capabilities.
  • OS review covers provenance, approved versions, program libraries, patch status, file permissions, startup services, administrative utilities, logs and recovery settings. A supported OS with a weak configuration can remain vulnerable.

4.6.3 to 4.6.4 Utilities, communications, licensing and source code

Utility programs can bypass ordinary application controls, so restrict them, authorize their use and independently review material activity.

Mode Behavior
Simplex One direction only
Half-duplex Alternates direction
Full-duplex Both directions at the same time

These describe transmission, not whether a business exchange is complete or secure. Reconcile transaction outcomes independently of transport success.

Reconcile licence rights with installation and actual use, including virtualized and cloud deployments. Keep source repositories controlled, and verify the relationship between source, builds and deployed objects. The right to commit code should not automatically confer unrestricted production deployment authority.

4.6.5 Capacity management

Forecast demand using business growth, seasonality, planned changes and measured bottlenecks. Monitor CPU, memory, storage, I/O, network, database connections, response time, throughput and queue depth, with thresholds and owners for action. Average utilization can hide short peaks or a single saturated dependency.

Example: a payment service has spare CPU but slow storage writes. Adding CPU may not help. Correlate business response times with I/O and queue metrics, validate the bottleneck and test a remedy. Likewise, CPU below 40 percent with rising latency may mean database locks are serializing the workload.

Autoscaling needs sensible limits, available resources and tested behavior; it can also amplify cost or fail to fix a downstream constraint.

Availability, MTBF and MTTR

Availability measures need a defined service boundary, observation window and downtime treatment.

  • Observed availability = (service window minus included downtime) / service window x 100. A 1,000-minute window with 10 minutes of downtime gives 99 percent.
  • A 99.9 percent target over 10,000 minutes allows 10 minutes of included downtime.
  • MTBF = total eligible operating time / number of failures. MTTR = total repair time / completed repairs. With 900 operating hours, 3 failures and 6 repair hours, MTBF is 300 hours and MTTR is 2 hours.
  • In a simplified model, availability is about MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). With 990 hours and 10 hours to repair, that is 99 percent.

MTTR is an average, not a guaranteed deadline for an individual outage. Zero observed failures do not prove infinite reliability. Establish the definitions before comparing providers; a contract may exclude maintenance, but the business impact still exists.

Auditor perspective: capacity during failover

Both processing sites are fine under normal demand, but after one fails the remaining site can serve only part of peak demand and queues grow. Compare actual throughput, latency and capacity under a representative failover with the service objectives, and inspect the assumptions about reserved capacity, dependencies and priority work. Use availability measures for the agreed window, and separately assess whether the surviving capacity keeps the critical service usable at the required load.

Practice questions

1. A payment service shows CPU utilization below 40 percent, yet response times are slow. What should the IS auditor do FIRST? Answer: correlate response times with I/O and queue metrics. Validate the bottleneck before recommending a remedy.

2. A vendor reports 99.9 percent availability, excluding scheduled maintenance, but users complain. What should the IS auditor do NEXT? Answer: compare the reporting definitions with user impact. Meeting the contract does not remove the business impact.

3. Both sites meet availability targets under normal demand. Which finding is MOST significant? Answer: the surviving site cannot carry peak demand. Capacity after failure is what the objectives depend on.
