CISA Domain 3.8: Postimplementation Review

CISA Domain 3.8: what a postimplementation review weighs, the evidence and baselines it uses, timing and independence, benefit ownership after closure, workarounds and pilots.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A procurement platform went live on time and under budget. It promised a twenty percent cut in handling time. It is stable, but manual exceptions have grown. Is the project a success? On the CISA exam, you cannot say yet. This lesson covers CISA Domain 3, Section 3.8: postimplementation review.

What a review is

A postimplementation review (PIR) evaluates the live result against business objectives, requirements and delivery commitments. It considers benefits, lifecycle costs, performance, adoption, control effectiveness, support and remaining risks. Meeting the launch date does not establish that the investment succeeded.

Review Final status meeting
Compares the delivered solution and outcomes with objectives, requirements and the business case Reports on delivery
Checks whether objectives and benefits were achieved Is not a budget check

A project can deliver the specified features within budget and still fail its business objective. Conversely, a justified approved change can make comparison with the obsolete baseline misleading, so compare against the baseline that is currently valid.

Evidence and baselines

Assess outcomes using evidence from users, operations, financial records and live controls. Check access, reconciliations, incidents, capacity, recovery, documentation and unresolved issues. Compare actual results with approved baselines, and distinguish cash savings from capacity or qualitative benefits.

Example: the procurement platform is stable, but manual exceptions have increased. Compare actual processing time with the baseline, investigate the causes of the exceptions and assess the control effects. Successful installation is not evidence of the promised benefit.

Preserve independence from the delivery decisions under review. The PIR should be led by someone independent of the project team.

Timing and ownership

Immediate stabilization and longer-term benefits evaluation serve different purposes. Schedule the review after enough activity has occurred to assess the relevant outcomes; there is no universal fixed interval, although urgent control failures should be checked promptly.

  • Assign actions, owners and dates.
  • Confirm accountable ownership of deferred items.
  • Retain benefit ownership after the project closes: benefits tracking starts in the business case and continues after closure.
  • Transfer residual work and risks to accountable owners with appropriate authority.
  • Feed lessons into architecture, procurement and future project governance.

Workarounds and pilots

Example: a new purchasing system was meant to reduce duplicate payments, but staff maintain an uncontrolled spreadsheet to bypass blocked transactions. Investigate requirements, usability, training, interfaces and workarounds before assigning a cause. A mismatch between specifications and business needs can indicate deficient requirements, so do not treat persistent workarounds automatically as a coding defect.

A pilot introduces a system to a limited, selected group or location before wider rollout. Compare the results with approved success criteria, record defects, control exceptions, reconciliation, user acceptance and support readiness, and assess whether the pilot population and load represent the wider rollout. Success in one small branch does not establish capacity, interfaces or recovery for the whole enterprise. The authorized business owner decides whether to expand, remediate or stop.

Auditor perspective: measure the live result

A claims platform was meant to shorten approval time. The online stage is faster, but claims wait in a manual queue before review. Measure end-to-end elapsed time using the approved baseline and comparable cases, examine adoption, service stability and control exceptions, and check the benefit owner's action on the queue. Completing the deployment milestone establishes the release event; the review explains the outcome and any corrective decision.

Practice questions

1. A new claims platform met its launch date and budget, and the sponsor describes the project as a success. What should the IS auditor do FIRST? Answer: compare live results with the approved objectives and business case. The review is not only a budget check.

2. A PIR finds that staff keep an uncontrolled spreadsheet to bypass transactions blocked by a new purchasing system. What should the IS auditor do FIRST? Answer: investigate requirements, usability, training and interfaces before assigning a cause.

3. An IS auditor is reviewing the results of a pilot at one small branch before the business owner decides on wider rollout. Which consideration is MOST important? Answer: whether the pilot population and load represent the wider rollout.
