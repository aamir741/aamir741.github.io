CISA Domain 2.11: Quality Assurance and Quality Management of IT

CISA Domain 2.11: quality assurance versus quality control, reviewer independence, the QMS and ISO 9001, TQM, PDCA, operational excellence and what auditors check.

Muhammad Aamir · 27 November 2026

A team tests ten released transactions, and every calculation is correct. Have you just assured the release process? On the CISA exam, no: that's quality control, not quality assurance. This lesson covers CISA Domain 2, Section 2.11: how quality assurance, quality control and quality management work together to deliver services and products that meet requirements consistently.

2.11 Three quality questions

Quality assurance, quality control and quality management work together, but they answer different questions. Getting the distinction right is the most common thing the exam tests here.

2.11.1 Quality assurance

Quality assurance (QA) consists of planned, systematic activities that provide confidence requirements will be met. It emphasizes the processes used to produce and deliver the result: method reviews, process audits and checking that required reviews occur. Quality control (QC) evaluates the output against requirements through inspection, testing or other checks.

Concept Focus Example
QA Confidence in the process and its conformance Do release procedures require and retain independent testing?
QC Whether the output meets requirements Does the released application calculate tax correctly?
QMS The overall quality management system Policies, objectives, roles, methods, records, improvement

The distinction concerns the purpose of the activity, not the team's job title. A QA department may coordinate product testing, but a test examining the delivered output still serves a quality-control purpose.

Independence and release quality: a quality reviewer should not approve their own work or hold an incompatible operational responsibility. QA arrangements can include standards, staff training, process reviews and checks that changes were authorized, tested and version-controlled before release. Small teams may combine roles but still need suitably independent review and evidence that it occurred.

2.11.2 Quality management

Quality management directs and controls the enterprise's quality activities. A quality management system (QMS) brings together policy, objectives, responsibilities, resources, processes, evaluation and improvement.

  • ISO 9001 is a QMS standard. Certification concerns its stated scope; it doesn't prove every output is defect-free.
  • Total quality management (TQM) emphasizes quality throughout the organization and continuing improvement with employee and customer involvement. Use feedback, defects, process reviews and root cause analysis to improve the system, rather than inspecting the same recurring defect every cycle.
  • Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) is an improvement cycle: plan an objective and change, implement it, evaluate results against expectations, then standardize an effective change or revise the approach.

Record owners and evidence of improvement. Closing an action because a document was issued does not show the recurring problem was corrected.

2.11.3 Operational excellence

Operational excellence sustains reliable, effective and efficient service through clear ownership, sound processes, appropriate controls and continuing improvement. It weighs customer outcomes, resilience, quality and cost together, and isn't achieved by minimizing headcount or automating an unstable process.

Example: a service desk cuts average ticket duration by repeatedly closing unresolved cases. The indicator improves while user effort and repeat incidents rise. An effective improvement measures successful resolution and recurrence, investigates the root cause, and adjusts the workflow and incentives.

Auditor perspective: activity versus outcome

Review the quality policy, standards, review independence, defect records, customer feedback, corrective actions and measured results. Trace a recurring issue through its cause, approved improvement and later evidence. Management owns quality; internal audit independently evaluates it.

Check whether quality staff can raise findings and get them resolved without suppression. Reporting lines can reveal threats, but actual authority, independent review and escalation decide whether challenge is protected. A development manager who can delete adverse test findings without review is more direct evidence of weakness than a job title alone.

Example: a team reports a 98 percent test pass rate while excluding all failed critical-security tests as optional. Inspect the requirements and defect population, severity rules, exclusions and acceptance authority. A higher maturity level is not certification that every application is secure.

Practice questions

1. A team tests ten released transactions and finds every calculation correct. The audit objective is to assess quality assurance over releases. Which additional work would BEST meet the objective? Answer: inspect how tests, defects and approvals are managed. A bigger sample is still quality control over outputs.

2. In a small team, one developer builds a change and also performs its quality review before release. What is the BEST recommendation? Answer: require an independent reviewer and keep evidence of the review. A job title or a signature doesn't remove self-review.
