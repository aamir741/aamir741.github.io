CISA Domain 4.7: Problem and Incident Management

CISA Domain 4.7: incident versus problem management, known errors, the incident handling process, escalation, the service desk, reporting reviews, security incidents, containment and chain of custody.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A nightly job fails and is restarted every morning, and the tickets close inside the target time. Is the service healthy? Not really. This lesson covers CISA Domain 4, Section 4.7: how an IS auditor separates restoring service from removing the cause, and how incidents are handled, escalated and reported.

4.7 Incident versus problem

Incident management restores normal service after an interruption or reduction in quality. Problem management investigates underlying causes and reduces recurrence or impact. A workaround may restore service without eliminating the cause, so the two processes should connect rather than compete.

4.7.1 Problem management

Analyze recurring or significant incidents, investigate causes, document known errors and workarounds, and manage permanent fixes through change control. A known error is an analyzed problem with documented information that supports resolution or a workaround; do not assume every ticket has a confirmed root cause.

Example: a nightly job fails and is restarted successfully each morning. Repeated failures and manual intervention warrant problem investigation. Confirm whether the cause is data, scheduling, capacity or code before choosing a fix.

4.7.2 Incident handling

  1. Detect and record the event.
  2. Assess impact and urgency.
  3. Prioritize and assign it.
  4. Diagnose, escalate and restore service.
  5. Validate recovery with relevant users.
  6. Close with accurate evidence.

Prioritize on business impact and urgency, not arrival order or the most vocal user. Major incidents need coordination, communications and timely decisions. Preserve the timeline, actions, ownership, service effects and links to changes or related problems.

4.7.3 to 4.7.4 Abnormal conditions and the service desk

Define thresholds for abnormal behavior and meaningful alert routing. Record timestamps, affected service, symptoms, severity, actions and outcome.

Avoid closing an incident solely because an alert disappeared when the business outcome remains unknown.

The service desk is the central contact for incidents and requests. Identity verification matters for password resets and other access-related requests. Network management tools provide status, traffic, fault and performance information; assess their coverage, secure management and response to alerts.

4.7.5 Problem management reporting reviews

Review aging, repeated failures, time to restore, unresolved major causes and completion of corrective actions. A reduction in ticket counts can reflect underreporting rather than improved service. Trace selected serious incidents through restoration, cause analysis, authorized fixes and evidence that recurrence or impact declined.

Security incidents and major disruptions

  • A security event is an observable occurrence involving computing assets.
  • An alert draws attention to activity that may need investigation; it can be erroneous or benign.
  • A security incident meets the adopted criteria for actual or imminent compromise, unauthorized harm or a relevant policy violation.

Establish criteria and authority before relying on a tool label. Repeated failed logins may be a harmless expired credential or an account takeover attempt; evidence that an unauthorized actor is exporting customer records supports urgent incident handling.

Response covers preparing roles and authority, assessing scope and severity, containing harmful activity, removing causes, recovering safely and learning from the event. These activities can overlap and repeat. Once an incident is confirmed, containment is the first priority, with life safety always overriding. The choice of containment action depends on operational consequences and authority. Preserve evidence where feasible, and let escalation and legally required communications run in parallel rather than wait for containment to finish.

The computer security incident response team (CSIRT) coordinates specialists. Chain of custody documents evidence identification, collection, handling, transfers and storage. Avoid unnecessary wiping or unrecorded changes. Detailed forensics is developed further in Domain 5.

Auditor perspective: restore service and investigate recurrence

A workaround can be appropriate for immediate restoration, but repeated failure needs problem investigation and an authorized lasting remedy. Do not confuse quick ticket closure with removal of the underlying cause.

Example: a service desk routes requests to an alternate queue to restore service, but the routing rule that caused the failure remains active for another group. The incident can close on restoration evidence. The problem stays open until the cause or remaining exposure is addressed and validated across the affected groups. Audit assesses the process and outcomes without taking over operational command or choosing unapproved emergency actions.

Practice questions

1. A nightly batch job fails and is restarted by operators each morning, and tickets close on target. What should the IS auditor recommend? Answer: opening a problem investigation into the recurring failure. A closed ticket is not evidence the cause was removed.

2. A security incident is confirmed and customer data is being exported. What should be the FIRST priority? Answer: containing the harmful activity, preserving evidence where feasible. Escalation and communications can run in parallel.

3. Incident tickets fell by 40 percent after a new service desk process. What should the IS auditor do NEXT? Answer: determine whether incidents are being under-reported. A drop in tickets is not proof of better service.
