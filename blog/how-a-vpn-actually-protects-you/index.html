How a VPN Actually Protects You (Explained Simply)

What a VPN really does on public Wi-Fi, explained with a simple postcard analogy, plus what a VPN can NOT protect you from.

Muhammad Aamir Â· 17 October 2026

You sit down in a café, connect to the free Wi-Fi and open your banking app. It feels harmless. But on a shared network, the people around you are, technically speaking, on the same road as your data. This is the problem a VPN (Virtual Private Network) solves.

Public Wi-Fi is like sending a postcard

When you send a postcard, anyone who handles it along the way can read it. Data travelling over an open or poorly secured Wi-Fi network can behave the same way: someone on that network, with the right tools, may be able to see where your traffic is going and, in weak setups, what it contains.

A VPN puts your postcard in a sealed envelope

A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN server. Everything you send is scrambled before it leaves your phone or laptop, so anyone watching the café network sees only gibberish travelling to one destination: the VPN server.

The VPN server then unwraps your request and visits the website on your behalf. That has a second effect: the website sees the VPN server's IP address, not your real one.

Without a VPN With a VPN
Traffic visible on the local network Traffic encrypted inside a tunnel
Websites see your real IP address Websites see the VPN server's address
Risky on public Wi-Fi Much safer on public Wi-Fi

Three things a VPN does for you

  1. Encrypts your data in transit, so snoopers on the same network see only scrambled data.
  2. Hides your IP address from the websites you visit.
  3. Makes public Wi-Fi safer, in cafés, airports and hotels.

The auditor's tip: what a VPN can NOT do

From an IT audit point of view, the most common mistake is treating a VPN as complete protection. It is not. A VPN protects data in transit. It does not stop:

  • Phishing: if you type your password into a fake website, the VPN will happily deliver it.
  • Malware already on your device.
  • Weak or reused passwords.

Use a VPN on public networks, but combine it with strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication.

How to choose a VPN

  • Avoid unknown free VPNs. If a VPN service is free, your data may be the product.
  • Choose a well-known provider that has been independently audited.
  • Look for a clear no-logs policy, meaning the provider does not record what you do.
  • At work, use only your organisation's approved VPN. Personal VPNs on company devices can break security controls and policies.

A VPN is your sealed envelope on the internet: simple, effective, and one layer of a sensible security routine.
