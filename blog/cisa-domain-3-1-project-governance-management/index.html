CISA Domain 3.1: Project Governance and Management

CISA Domain 3.1: governance versus management, project roles and structure, estimating and scheduling techniques, earned value, benefits realization and the auditor's role.

Muhammad Aamir · 27 November 2026

A project reports green on schedule, but only because delayed acceptance work was moved out of scope. Is that success? On the CISA exam, no. This lesson covers CISA Domain 3, Section 3.1: project governance and management.

Domain 3 and why governance comes first

Domain 3, Information Systems Acquisition, Development and Implementation, carries 12 percent of the exam. It follows the decisions that stop a system from being installed successfully and still failing the business. Keep the decision makers separate: developers build, users establish business acceptance, and authorized management accepts residual risk. The auditor evaluates and advises while preserving independence.

Project governance establishes authority, oversight and accountability. Project management organizes the work within those boundaries. A detailed schedule cannot compensate for an investment that has lost its business justification.

A project is temporary and delivers a defined result, and its scope, time, cost, quality, resources and risks interact. Management activities (initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, closing) are not the same thing as technical SDLC phases.

3.1.2 and 3.1.3 Structure and roles

  • Functional: staff report to departments, so project authority may be limited.
  • Projectized: the project manager directs a dedicated team.
  • Matrix: both relationships combine, so priorities and escalation need clarity.

Assess actual authority in use, not only the organization chart.

Role Primary responsibility
Sponsor Owns the business justification and resolves escalated business issues
Steering committee Oversight, stage gates and major changes
Project manager Coordinates delivery, dependencies and status reporting
Business owner Defines needs, owns business controls, accepts suitability
IS auditor Evaluates evidence without owning or approving the project

3.1.4 to 3.1.10 Techniques and planning

Use a work breakdown structure for scope, a dependency network for sequencing, a schedule for timing, a risk register for uncertain events and an issue log for problems that have occurred. Plan-driven, adaptive and hybrid methods are all acceptable if they give accountability and evidence appropriate to the risk.

  • Estimates must include testing, migration, training, documentation, controls and the operating transition.
  • Function point analysis measures functional size; function points are not staff hours. COCOMO estimates effort from size and cost drivers.
  • The critical path is the longest dependent path; total float is the delay an activity can absorb. In the book's example (A 2 days, B 4, C 3, D 2, E 1), A-B-D-E is 9 days and critical, and C has 1 day of float.
  • PERT expected duration is (O + 4M + P) / 6. With 2, 5 and 8 days, that is 5 days.
  • A timebox fixes time and flexes scope through the authorized process, never by quietly removing testing or controls.

3.1.12 Monitoring and earned value

Compare actual progress, cost, quality and risk with the approved baseline, and distinguish completed work from effort spent.

Measure Formula
Schedule variance (SV) EV - PV
Cost variance (CV) EV - AC
Schedule performance index (SPI) EV / PV
Cost performance index (CPI) EV / AC
Estimate at completion (EAC) BAC / CPI, if current efficiency continues
Estimate to complete (ETC) EAC - AC

Example: PV 120,000, EV 90,000, AC 100,000 gives SV -30,000, SPI 0.75, CV -10,000 and CPI 0.90. The project delivered less than planned and spent more than it earned. Schedule variance is in value units, not days.

Portfolio, PMO, benefits and closure

A portfolio is a collection of investments for strategic objectives; a program coordinates related projects for joint benefits. A PMO may be supportive, controlling or directive, and its mandate should be documented. Benefits realization needs a baseline, target, measure, timing and owner, and continues after closure: installed servers are an output, reduced processing time is an outcome. A charter authorizes the project but does not replace the plan or business case, and administrative closure does not prove benefits occurred.

Auditor perspective

Assess the business case, roles, reporting, risk management, changes and quality gates; report concerns early; do not manage the project, select the solution or accept residual risk. When cost or scope changes materially, revalidate the business case before any rescue action. If unfinished work is moved out of scope to report green, trace the change to the sponsor's approved objectives and trace earned value to completed deliverables.

Practice questions

1. A project is 40 percent over budget, and a key regulatory requirement has been dropped from scope. What should the IS auditor recommend FIRST? Answer: reassess whether the business case remains valid. Rescue actions presume the project should continue.

2. A team is falling behind in a timeboxed iteration. Which action is MOST appropriate? Answer: defer lower-value items through the product owner. A timebox fixes time and quality and flexes scope.

3. Planned value is 120,000, earned value is 90,000 and actual cost is 100,000. Which conclusion is BEST supported? Answer: behind schedule and over cost for work done. Schedule variance is in value units, not days.
