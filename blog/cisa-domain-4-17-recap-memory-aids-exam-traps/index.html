CISA Domain 4.17: Domain 4 Recap, Memory Aids and Exam Traps

CISA Domain 4.17: the Domain 4 last mile with memory aids, exam traps, decision owners and control purposes for IT operations and business resilience.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A shared server supports three applications, with recovery time objectives of two, eight and twenty four hours. Someone proposes setting the server's target at the average. Is that right? On the exam, the tempting answer is usually wrong. This lesson covers CISA Domain four, section 4.17: the last mile. Memory aids, exam traps, decision owners and control purposes.

Memory aids

Memory aids first. Use them to organize recall after you understand the explanations, and always apply the facts and reasoning from the chapter, not just the phrase.

Know it, own it, control it: inventory the asset, assign accountability and apply lifecycle controls. Incident restores, problem investigates the cause, because a workaround can restore service while a permanent fix is outstanding. Trace from reality: to find unapproved changes, start with reliable evidence of actual changes and trace to authorization. And customer, colleagues, contractor: an S L A with the customer, an O L A between internal teams, and an underpinning contract with a supplier.

  • Know it, own it, control it: Inventory, accountability, lifecycle controls
  • Incident restores: Problem investigates the cause
  • Trace from reality: Start with actual changes
  • Customer, colleagues, contractor: SLA, OLA, underpinning contract

Time, point, capability: R T O is recovery time, R P O is the recoverable data point, and S D O is the required alternate service. Need, strategy, plan, prove, improve: the B I A identifies need, strategy chooses a route, plans make it executable, exercises provide evidence, and follow up improves it. And copies must survive together failures, so separate the relevant physical, administrative and cyber failure domains.

  • Time, point, capability: RTO, RPO, SDO
  • Need, strategy, plan, prove, improve: BIA first
  • Copies must survive: Together-failures

Full plus chain, full plus latest difference: an incremental restore needs the required chain, while a differential restore needs the suitable full and the latest differential. RAID keeps disks going, backup keeps recovery options. Recover the service, not just the server, including identities, network, data, interfaces and business validation. And route names don't prove route independence, so check conduits, entry points, exchanges, providers and power.

  • Full plus chain: Full plus latest difference
  • RAID keeps disks going: Backup keeps options
  • Recover the service: Not just the server
  • Route names: Don't prove independence

Test all objectives: Test all the objectives. Time, data currency, integrity, functionality and alternate capacity all matter. A scheduler's success, a backup job or RAID protection doesn't alone establish a complete business result.

Calculation Check: Before calculating availability, define uptime, downtime, failures and repair intervals. The formula is easy once the inputs are agreed, and the exam usually hides the trap in the inputs.

Exam traps: change and incidents

Now the exam traps. Each pairs a tempting wrong answer with the supported response.

Trap: Audit Logging: The tempting answer is to choose audit logging as the best control for data integrity. Selected audit logs support accountability and detection, but database transaction logs also support commit, rollback and recovery. Assess the specific log and its control purpose.

Trap: Emergency Change: The tempting answer is to treat an emergency change deployed before approval as a violation. Instead, use the defined emergency authority, and obtain authorization beforehand where feasible. Preserve actions and decisions, and complete independent review and documentation promptly afterward.

Trap: The Change Register: A complete change register doesn't prove there were no unauthorized changes. Start from actual production changes and trace them back to approved records.

Trap: Restarting Jobs: Restarting a failing job repeatedly to close incidents is a trap. Incident management restores service, but recurring failures need problem management to remove the root cause.

Exam traps: continuity and recovery

Next, the recovery traps.

Trap: Start With A Site: The tempting answer is to start continuity planning by selecting a recovery site or writing the plan. Once sponsorship, policy and scope exist, use the B I A and owner input to establish criticality, dependencies and approved recovery requirements, before selecting arrangements.

Trap: Rto Proves Recovery: Meeting R T O proves recovery? No. Recovery is effective when the R T O is met, the data is within the R P O, and the required functions are validated.

Trap: Rto Beyond Tolerance: Accepting an R T O longer than the maximum tolerable downtime is a trap. Recovery targets must fit the approved interruption tolerance, with time for necessary validation. Compare the actual scenario's exposures, rather than applying a universal ranking.

Trap: Schedule Vs Rpo: A fifteen minute backup schedule doesn't guarantee a fifteen minute R P O. The R P O drives backup frequency, but only successful, recoverable copies meet it.

Trap: Replication: Replication is not a backup. Replication and RAID copy deletion and corruption, so keep independent offsite backups.

Trap: A Hot Site For Everything: Choosing a hot site for every system, regardless of its R T O, is a trap. Match demonstrated end to end recovery capability, preparation, capacity, dependencies and total cost to the approved business need. Site labels don't establish fixed hours.

Trap: Two Carriers: Two carriers eliminate telecommunications risk? Verify the actual alternate and physically diverse paths, including shared ducts, entrances, exchanges, providers and power.

Trap: The Most Critical Element: Naming the recovery site as the most critical D R element is a trap. Protected recoverable data is essential where restoration depends on it. Also assess activation authority, resources, dependencies and timing.

Trap: Stale Contact List: Ranking a stale contact list above a structural plan failure is a trap. Assess objective feasibility and activation authority, then compare consequences and likelihood using the stated facts. A stale contact can also be critical where no working alternative exists.

Trap: Shared Server Rto: Setting a shared server's R T O at the average of its applications is a trap. It must be ready early enough for the earliest dependent service, including subsequent validation, unless a tested alternative removes that dependency.

Trap: Document Review: Relying on a document review to prove the D R plan works is a trap. Execution tests demonstrate capability within their scope, conditions and time. Discussion and document reviews identify gaps, but do not establish technical recovery.

Before you answer: Before you answer, ask what the scenario actually demonstrates. Is it a label, a schedule, a register or a document, or is it real, tested recovery? Then choose the answer that rests on evidence, and remember that the auditor assesses while management decides.

Decision owners

Now the decision owners at a glance. Use the assigned authority and evidence to distinguish delivery responsibility from oversight and assurance.

The business process owner defines criticality, impacts and acceptable recovery capability. The IT or service owner delivers operation and recoverability against approved needs. The crisis or invocation authority makes plan activation and major response decisions. And recovery teams execute, validate, reconcile and hand back operation.

  • Business process owner: Defines criticality and recovery capability
  • IT or service owner: Delivers recoverability
  • Crisis authority: Plan activation decisions
  • Recovery teams: Execute, validate, hand back

Boundaries: Each role has a boundary. Technical convenience shouldn't set business tolerance. Restore commands alone don't prove a recovered service. Named deputies must also be able to act. And failback needs the same control of data and authority as the recovery itself.

Control purposes

Finally, threats and control purpose. The examples show complementary purposes, and classification depends on how a control is configured and used.

For recurring service failure, preventive direction is a supported design, capacity and controlled changes. Detective evidence is operational alerts and incident and problem trends. And corrective direction is to restore service and remove the investigated cause.

  • Preventive: Supported design, capacity, controlled changes
  • Detective: Alerts and incident and problem trends
  • Corrective: Restore service, remove the cause

For loss of primary processing, preventive direction is independent dependencies and a suitable recovery strategy. Detective evidence is realistic recovery exercises and backup monitoring. And corrective direction is to recover, reconcile and validate the required service.

  • Preventive: Independent dependencies, suitable strategy
  • Detective: Realistic exercises, backup monitoring
  • Corrective: Recover, reconcile, validate

Practice questions

Test yourself with three exam-style questions. To recap domain four. Test every objective, not just the R T O. Prove independence of paths and copies. Trace from reality, not from the register. And remember that incident management restores, while problem management removes the cause.

  • Test every objective: Time, data, function
  • Prove independence: Paths and copies
  • Trace from reality: Not from the register
  • Incident vs problem: Restore, then remove the cause

Practice questions

1. An IS auditor wants to find unauthorized production changes. Which approach is BEST? Answer: Start from actual production changes and trace them to approvals. Option A, reviewing the register, can't find changes that never entered it. Option B samples approved requests, so it only confirms what was already approved. And option D gathers opinions, not evidence of what actually changed.

2. A second site holds a replica of all production data. What should the IS auditor conclude about independent backups? Answer: Independent offsite backups remain needed, since replication copies deletion. Option B treats the replica as a backup, but it copies deletion and corruption. Option C reduces backups because of replication, which has the same flaw. And option D limits backups to systems without RAID, but RAID is not a backup either.

3. A job fails nightly and operators restart it to close each incident. What should the IS auditor recommend? Answer: Raise a problem record so the root cause is investigated and removed. Option A automates the symptom, and the failure continues. Option B raises closure targets so restarts look like fixes, which hides the problem. And option C adds a second operator, which restarts faster but removes no cause.
