CISA Domain 5.2: Physical and Environmental Controls

CISA Domain 5.2: environmental exposures, power and fire suppression, physical access layers, mantraps and tailgating, auditing physical access and industrial control systems.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A data center has biometric readers on every door. Yet staff keep holding the door open for colleagues walking in behind them. Does the biometric reader still protect the room? This lesson covers CISA Domain five, section 5.2: physical and environmental controls.

5.2 Physical and environmental

Physical controls restrict access to assets. Environmental controls protect them against conditions such as heat, water, fire and power interruption.

Scope of the evaluation: Evaluate both through the business impact and the actual facility design. And protect people and emergency egress as well as equipment. A control that traps staff in a fire is not a good control, however secure the room looks.

Tempest: T E M P E S T concerns the investigation and control of unintended emanations from information processing equipment that could reveal information. Distinguish information leakage from ordinary interference or loss of power, and use specialist requirements where the risk warrants them.

5.2.1 Environmental exposures

Assess power quality and continuity, cooling, humidity, water ingress, fire, contamination, cabling and equipment placement. Use appropriate detection, alarms, maintenance and escalation.

  • Power: Quality and continuity
  • Cooling and humidity: Within equipment limits
  • Water ingress: Detection and routing
  • Fire: Detection and suppression
  • Cabling and placement: Contamination too

Ups and generators: A U P S provides power continuity within its capacity and duration. A generator supports longer outages only if startup, fuel, switching and maintenance arrangements work. Both need testing, not just installation, because an untested generator is only a hope.

Common source trap: Review common dependencies. Two power supplies connected to the same failed source do not provide independent supply. And environmental alarms need a timely response, not only a dashboard.

Fire Suppression: Fire detection and suppression require a professionally engineered system suited to occupancy, equipment and applicable safety requirements. Clean agents, inert gases and water based systems have different constraints. Review testing, maintenance, alarms, release procedures and coordination with facility specialists.

Carbon dioxide is used as a gaseous suppression agent, and its suitability and life safety design require qualified assessment. Halon is a family of fire extinguishing agents found in legacy systems, so consider environmental restrictions and approved replacement arrangements. And a wet pipe sprinkler has piping that normally contains water. Distinguish it from dry pipe or preaction arrangements, rather than declaring one system best for every facility.

  • CO2: Gaseous agent, life-safety design needed
  • Halon: Legacy agent, environmental restrictions
  • Wet-pipe sprinkler: Piping normally holds water

Temperature and epo: Monitor temperature and humidity against equipment and facility requirements, and protect water sensitive areas with detection and suitable routing. Emergency power off, or E P O, removes electrical power from specified equipment or areas. Evaluate its scope, accessibility, protection against accidental activation and approved life safety design.

5.2.2 Physical access

Use layers such as perimeter controls, staffed reception, verified credentials, restricted areas, controlled doors, cabinets and monitoring. Visitors need approval, identity checks, appropriate escort and access expiration, so a visitor badge doesn't quietly become a permanent one.

  • Perimeter: And staffed reception
  • Verified credentials: Restricted areas
  • Controlled doors: Cabinets
  • Monitoring: Evidence and deterrence

No perfect control: Badges can be lost, shared or cloned. Biometrics can be spoofed or reject valid users. CCTV can deter and provide evidence, but needs suitable placement, retention, review and privacy controls. The strongest control depends on the threat, and visitor escort doesn't replace reliable control of unattended entry points.

Piggybacking: Piggybacking, also called tailgating, occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized person through a controlled door without presenting a credential. It defeats badge and biometric readers, because the control authenticates only the first person. Courtesy, social pressure and propped doors make it common. That's why a locked door with a reader can still be an open door in practice.

A mantrap, also called a deadman door or interlocking entrance, uses two doors so that only one person can pass after authenticating. The second door won't open until the first has closed. Anti passback rules, turnstiles, guards at high risk entries, camera review and staff awareness support it.

  • Two doors: One person passes at a time
  • Second door waits: Until the first closes
  • Support controls: Anti-passback, turnstiles, guards
  • CCTV and awareness: Reviewed and trained

Exam Reasoning: An interlocking vestibule directly addresses following through, when occupancy sensing and operation enforce single person passage. Examine bypasses, exceptions and life safety behavior. Stronger credentials alone do not stop an unauthenticated follower.

5.2.3 Auditing physical access

Observe actual entry and exit practices. Reconcile access rights to authorized personnel. Inspect visitor handling. And test the removal of obsolete access.

  • Observe: Actual entry and exit
  • Reconcile: Access rights to authorized people
  • Inspect visitors: Handling and escorts
  • Test removal: Of obsolete access

Broader Scope: Review alarms, exceptions, forced door events and restricted area access. Sample the exceptions, because that's where the real access decisions hide. Include contractors, cleaners, delivery routes and emergency procedures. And compare records with actual conditions, because a signed policy does not prove consistent enforcement.

5.2.4 Industrial control systems

What ics is: Industrial control systems, including SCADA and programmable logic controllers, monitor or control physical processes. Safety, availability, timing and equipment constraints can dominate decisions. A control appropriate for an office laptop may disrupt a production process.

Inventory assets and dependencies. Segment operational technology from other networks. Restrict remote access, and use controlled gateways or industrial D M Zs. And monitor appropriately and maintain recoverable configurations.

  • Inventory: Assets and dependencies
  • Segment: OT from other networks
  • Restrict remote access: Controlled gateways, industrial DMZ
  • Recoverable configurations: Monitor appropriately

Patching and scanning: Coordinate changes and patching with engineering, safety and operations. Use tested compensating controls when immediate patching is impractical. And remember that active scanning or testing needs authorization and an assessment of process impact.

Auditor perspective

Now the auditor perspective: testing physical boundaries and environmental dependencies.

Badge Policy: Evaluate the actual paths, including deliveries, maintenance, emergency exits and shared spaces. A badge policy cannot compensate for an uncontrolled door through which people routinely follow authorized staff. And two interlocked doors alone do not guarantee one person at a time, so examine exceptions, accessible entry and emergency behavior.

Redundant Feeds: Redundant power feeds may share the same distribution unit and maintenance procedure. Verify the actual common cause and tested transfer behavior, rather than counting feeds. Audit should not prescribe one suppression technology as universally correct for every facility.

Physical protection includes site and room boundaries, identity verification, visitors, escorts, restricted zones, monitoring and timely removal of access. Evaluate the actual paths, including deliveries, maintenance, emergency exits and shared spaces, because attackers and accidents use the paths people really take.

  • Site and room boundaries: Identity verification
  • Visitors and escorts: Restricted zones
  • Monitoring: Timely removal of access
  • Actual paths: Deliveries, exits, shared spaces

Provider Evidence: Environmental protection includes suitable power, cooling, fire detection and suppression, water exposure and equipment conditions. Review inspection, maintenance, alarms, response ownership and provider evidence. Select safeguards for the site's risks and obligations.

Logged but not seen: A monitored door opens during a maintenance visit. The system logs the event, but the monitoring team receives no notification, because that alarm class was disabled for a prior job. Review authorizations, temporary overrides, restoration to normal settings and response records. An event in the log supports detection only when the appropriate people or system receive and act on it.

Physical and logical together: Test physical and logical access records together where both are relevant to sensitive administration. And use authorized exercises that respect the site's safety procedures. The auditor never tests a door by breaking safety rules.

Practice questions

Test yourself with three exam-style questions. To recap. Tailgating defeats badge and biometric readers, so use interlocking entry with occupancy sensing. Count common causes, not feeds. Compare records with reality, because a signed policy doesn't prove enforcement. And detection needs a response, since a log entry alone isn't enough.

  • Tailgating beats readers: Use interlocking doors
  • Count common causes: Not feeds
  • Compare records to reality: Not a signed policy
  • Detection needs a response: A log alone isn't enough

Practice questions

1. Staff routinely hold doors open for colleagues at a data center with badge readers. Which control BEST addresses this following-through? Answer: An interlocking vestibule with occupancy sensing and single-person passage. Option A strengthens credentials, but the reader still authenticates only the first person. Option B, visitor escort, doesn't replace control of an unattended entry point. And option D, camera footage, provides evidence after the event but doesn't stop the follower.

2. A data center's two power feeds share the same distribution unit and maintenance procedure. What should the IS auditor do? Answer: Verify the actual common cause and the tested transfer behavior. Option B accepts the feeds because they're labeled separately, but the common unit remains. Option C adds a third feed from the same unit, which adds nothing. And option D reports no finding because of the U P S, which provides continuity only within its capacity and duration.

3. A door alarm opened during maintenance and was logged, but monitoring staff were never notified because that alarm class had been disabled. What is the MOST significant concern? Answer: The event was logged, but nobody able to act was ever notified. Option A, log retention, is secondary. Option B, the badge identifying the contractor, doesn't explain the missed response. And option C, camera coverage, isn't the gap here either. A log entry supports detection only when someone receives and acts on it.
