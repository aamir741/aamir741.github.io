CISA Domain 5.3: Identity and Access Management

CISA Domain 5.3: identity and access management lifecycle, authentication and authorization, zero trust, PAM, access control models, SSO and federation, and biometrics.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

A finance employee moves to procurement and keeps the right to create suppliers and approve payments. Every login is correct. So where is the control failure? This lesson covers CISA Domain five, section 5.3: identity and access management.

In this lesson

  • Lifecycle: Joiners, movers, leavers
  • Authentication: Factors and authorization
  • Zero trust and PAM: Decisions and enforcement
  • Models and SSO: DAC, MAC, RBAC, federation
  • Biometrics: FAR, FRR and the bypass test

5.3.1 IAM lifecycle

Identity and access management decides who or what receives access, and keeps that access right throughout its life. That covers employees, contractors, customers, services, applications and devices.

Exam Trap: A correct login is only one part of access control.

Four ideas in order

  • Identity proofing: Establish who they are
  • Identification: Claim an identity
  • Authentication: Prove control of the authenticator
  • Authorization: Permitted actions only

Joiners, movers, leavers

  • Joiner: Approved provisioning
  • Mover: Remove what the old role needed
  • Leaver: Revoke everything, promptly
  • Authoritative source: HR or business records drive it

Recertification: Periodic recertification detects drift. It does not replace prompt removal.

Real-World Example: The login works, yet incompatible duties remain.

5.3.2 Authentication and authorization

Authentication vs authorization

  • Authentication: Does the claimant control the authenticator?; Assess enrollment and recovery
  • Authorization: Only permitted actions on permitted resources; Test server-side enforcement

Limit access three ways

  • Least privilege: Only what the task needs
  • Need-to-know: Justified business need
  • Segregation of duties: Separate, or compensate

Seniority is not need: A high clearance or senior role does not authorize all information.

Authentication factors

  • Something known: A password or PIN
  • Something possessed: A cryptographic device
  • Something inherent: A biometric trait

Exam Trap: Two passwords are still one factor type.

Password Storage: Use a salted password-hashing function, not reversible encryption.

5.3.3 Zero trust and PAM

Sections 5.3.3 and 5.3.4: zero trust, and privileged access management.

Zero Trust: Network location alone never grants broad access.

Decide, establish, enforce

  • Policy engine: Decides from policy and signals
  • Policy administrator: Opens or closes the path
  • Enforcement point: Applies the decision

Real-World Example: A decision at the gateway proves only that path.

Privileged access controls

  • Approval: Before elevation
  • Vaulting and rotation: Protect the credentials
  • Time-limited: Elevation that expires
  • Monitoring: Independent review of sessions

A vault is not a cure: A vault does not fix excessive rights on a service account.

5.3.5 Models, SSO and federation

Now the access control models, then single sign-on and federation.

Access control models

  • DAC: Owners grant rights
  • MAC: Central labels and clearances
  • RBAC: Rights through roles
  • ABAC: Subject, resource, context attributes

Federation protocols

  • SSO: One login, many applications
  • SAML: Authentication and attribute assertions
  • OAuth 2.0: Delegated authorization
  • OpenID Connect: Adds an identity layer

Exam Trap: OAuth 2.0 is not by itself an authentication protocol.

Kerberos: The KDC is critical, but not inevitably a single point of failure.

Remote and third parties: Contracts help. Actual access controls still need verifying.

5.3.8 Biometrics

Last, biometrics, and how to audit logical access. Biometrics uses physical or behavioral characteristics such as fingerprints, iris patterns or typing behavior.

Biometric measures

  • FAR: Impostor wrongly accepted
  • FRR: Genuine user wrongly rejected
  • CER or EER: Where the two rates are equal

Type i and type ii: False rejection is Type I. False acceptance is Type II.

FAR vs FRR

  • FAR: Admits an impostor; Security risk
  • FRR: Locks out valid users; Availability risk

Enrollment: FER measures enrollment, not a rejected genuine user.

Auditor View: Test the bypass, not just the login.

Practice questions

1. An employee transferred from finance to procurement and still holds supplier creation and payment approval rights. The account authenticates correctly. What is the auditor's MOST significant concern? - A. Whether the employee's password meets the length policy - B. Whether the quarterly recertification was completed on time - C. Retained entitlements that now create incompatible duties - D. Whether multi-factor authentication is enabled on the account Answer: C. The account already authenticates; the failure is retained, incompatible authorization after a role change.

2. An enterprise is selecting a fingerprint reader for a high-security data centre door. Which measure is MOST relevant to preventing unauthorized entry? - A. False acceptance rate at the chosen threshold - B. False rejection rate at the chosen threshold - C. Failure-to-enroll rate for eligible employees - D. Overall accuracy stated as a single percentage Answer: A. False acceptance directly admits an impostor, so it matters most for preventing unauthorized entry.

3. A device fails its compliance check after login, but a privileged session keeps running through a direct administrative route. What should the auditor do FIRST? - A. Confirm that the VPN encrypts the administrative session end to end - B. Recommend longer password rules for administrator accounts - C. Accept the gateway's successful decision as sufficient proof - D. Trace the decision to enforcement on each path and test revocation Answer: D. A decision at one gateway is evidence only for that path; trace enforcement on every route and test revocation.
