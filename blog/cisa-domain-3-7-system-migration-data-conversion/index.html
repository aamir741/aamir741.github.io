CISA Domain 3.7: System Migration, Infrastructure Deployment and Data Conversion

CISA Domain 3.7: data migration and conversion, verifying completeness and accuracy, fallback, changeover techniques, promotion, training, certification and accreditation.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

Two migrated balances are wrong, yet the record count and the total both match. This lesson covers CISA Domain 3, Section 3.7: system migration, infrastructure deployment and data conversion, and why counts and totals are not proof.

3.7 Migration, conversion and changeover

Technology deployment, data migration, data conversion and business changeover are related but distinct, and each needs ownership and evidence. The transition should preserve service, information integrity and control accountability.

Migration moves data from a source to a target. Conversion changes its representation, structure, coding or meaning. Identify data owners and approve mappings, transformations, exclusions and validation before execution. Inventory data and interfaces, cleanse through authorized rules, extract, securely transfer and load in sequence, rehearse, control script and mapping versions, and reconcile exceptions. Temporary adapters need clear ownership and removal plans.

Verifying completeness and accuracy

  • Completeness: source population, unique identifiers, counts, relevant totals and explained exclusions.
  • Accuracy: significant field comparisons and approved transformations, keys and relationships, and transactions since the extract.

Worked example: source balances A = $100 and B = $200; target balances A = $150 and B = $150. Both hold two records totaling $300, yet both balances are wrong. Counts and totals are useful, not sufficient. Remember: count + value + identity + meaning. Equal counts can conceal a missing record replaced by a duplicate, and equal balances can conceal offsetting errors. Protect temporary extracts, credentials and retained copies.

Fallback

Fallback restores an acceptable operating state when transition criteria fail. Define triggers, authority, steps, dependencies and timing, test the ability to restore (not just the existence of backups), and determine the point after which forward recovery is required. Restoring an earlier backup may discard transactions entered after go-live; use controlled journals, reverse conversion, replay or another tested method, and reconcile for omissions and duplicates.

3.7.2 Changeover techniques

Technique Main trade-off
Parallel Better comparison and continuity; more workload, cost and reconciliation
Phased Smaller changes; temporary interfaces and dependencies
Pilot Limits initial exposure; must represent wider conditions
Direct (abrupt) Concentrates risk; fallback exists only if designed and tested

Choose by business impact, architecture, interfaces, cost and recovery feasibility. No approach removes the need for testing, acceptance or fallback planning.

3.7.3 to 3.7.5 Promotion, training and authorization

Promote approved artifacts through controlled environments, separate incompatible duties, retain approval and deployment evidence and reconcile release records to actual versions. Keep a release manifest, dependencies, instructions, backup, fallback and verification criteria, and monitor controls after deployment. Train users in the changed process, controls and error handling; attendance proves participation, task demonstrations prove competence. Investigate workarounds before assigning cause.

For system software, verify compatibility, capacity, patches, security settings and licensing, preserve the prior state, and validate installation and recovery.

Certification assesses whether system controls meet specified requirements; accreditation is the formal management authorization to operate and acceptance of residual risk. The assessor provides evidence; the designated authority makes the risk decision.

Auditor perspective

Define the cutoff, identify initial and incremental populations, and trace late changes through mapping, load and reconciliation. Check duplicates, rejects and unresolved differences before business sign-off, and confirm the fallback trigger and evidence. Before reverting, determine what happened after cutover and how those transactions will be preserved and reconciled.

Practice questions

1. A hospital group is replacing its billing system. The vendor supports running both systems for three months, and billing errors would delay claims. Which changeover approach is MOST appropriate? Answer: parallel operation with output reconciliation for a period. Phasing adds interfaces, direct cutover concentrates risk, and a small pilot may not represent full volume.

2. Which reconciliation procedure provides the LEAST assurance that migrated customer balances are accurate? Answer: matching record counts and the total value only. Counts and totals can hide swapped or offsetting balances.

3. A migration team plans to revert to last night's backup if cutover fails, but the new system has already processed transactions. What should the IS auditor recommend? Answer: establish how post-cutover transactions are preserved and reconciled. A backup alone can discard valid business activity.
