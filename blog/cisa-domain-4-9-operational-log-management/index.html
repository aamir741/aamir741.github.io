CISA Domain 4.9: Operational Log Management

CISA Domain 4.9: log types, the log management cycle, time synchronization, protecting logs, WORM storage, SIEM, and assessing whether logs can support a conclusion.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

The security dashboard shows no suspicious activity. Does that mean nothing happened? Not necessarily. This lesson covers CISA Domain 4, Section 4.9: how an IS auditor decides whether logs are complete, protected and acted on, and whether they can support a conclusion.

4.9 Why logs matter

Logs record events such as access, changes, processing and failures. They support accountability, investigation, monitoring and evidence. Distinguish an audit trail from a database transaction log: transaction logs also support commit, rollback and recovery, so the statement "logs never support integrity" is too broad. A large volume of logs is not useful if the critical identity or event is missing.

4.9.1 Types of logs

Log What it records
Application Business or application events
System Operating behavior
Security Access and security events
Database audit Selected database actions
Transaction What the database needs for recovery

Define what to capture according to risk. Collect sufficient context: time, source, actor, action, affected object and result, without unnecessarily recording secrets or sensitive payloads.

4.9.2 Log management

The cycle covers generation, collection, transfer, storage, protection, analysis, alerting, reporting, retention and disposal. Synchronize time sources and document time zones. Detect collection failures, dropped events, parser problems and storage exhaustion. The absence of an alert means little if the relevant source stopped sending events.

Storing, protecting and analyzing logs

Restrict access, separate privileged system administration from independent review where practical, and use appropriately protected centralized storage. Append-only or immutable mechanisms can strengthen protection, but configuration, retention and administrative access still matter. Describe controls as tamper-resistant and verifiable rather than absolutely tamper-proof.

WORM (write once, read many) storage permits writing under defined rules and then reading, while restricting alteration or deletion for the configured retention period. Verify actual retention and administrative bypass controls, and do not confuse it with a self-propagating malware worm.

Tune meaningful alerts, assign owners and review outcomes. Correlation connects events across systems. False positives can overwhelm responders and false negatives can hide incidents. A SIEM centralizes and analyzes events; it does not replace ownership or response. Report unresolved concerns and trends to the appropriate governance level.

Auditor perspective: can the logs support a conclusion?

Log management includes identifying required events, collecting them reliably, synchronizing time, preserving integrity, controlling access, retaining evidence and responding to exceptions. Required coverage depends on risk and purpose.

Example: an administrator can delete local events before collection, and the central system does not alert on missing data. A dashboard showing no suspicious activity gives weak assurance. Test sample events from origin through collection, parsing, storage, correlation and review, including failures. Confirm time sources, access segregation, tamper resistance and retrieval capability, and obtain evidence that someone acts on relevant events, not simply that the collector is running.

Timeline example: an access event appears before its approval, but one source records local time and the other uses coordinated universal time. Confirm time zones, synchronization and clock drift before deciding the order. Preserve original timestamps, document the normalization, trace records to a complete source, and check whether collectors detect missing events.

Practice questions

1. An administrator can delete local events before collection and nothing alerts on missing data. The dashboard shows no suspicious activity. What should the IS auditor conclude? Answer: the dashboard assurance is weak because missing events would go undetected.

2. An access event appears before its approval, but the log sources use different time bases. What should the IS auditor do FIRST? Answer: confirm time zones, synchronization and any clock drift before deciding the order.

3. An enterprise has deployed a SIEM. Which factor is MOST important for effective monitoring? Answer: alerts are tuned, owned and followed up with reviewed outcomes. A SIEM does not replace ownership or response.
