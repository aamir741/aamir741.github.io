CISA Domain 4.15: Business Continuity Plan

CISA Domain 4.15: BCP versus disaster recovery, disruptive events, the planning process, plan components, insurance, testing, maintenance and the audit of business continuity.

Muhammad Aamir · 28 November 2026

The recovery application works perfectly at the alternate site. But nobody there is authorized to release urgent payments. Did the business recover? This lesson covers CISA Domain four, section 4.15: the business continuity plan.

4.15 What a BCP is

The business continuity plan, or B C P, supports priority business activities through disruption.

It covers people, premises, suppliers, processes, communications and technology.

  • People: Teams and deputies
  • Premises: Facilities and sites
  • Suppliers: Key dependencies
  • Processes: Priority activities
  • Communications: Stakeholders
  • Technology: Supports the rest

Exam Trap: Disaster recovery plans support the technology recovery needs of the B C P. They don't replace it. If a scenario describes only a technical plan, the business-wide plan may still be missing. Ask who runs the business while the technology is being recovered.

Who owns it: Senior management sponsors continuity, and business owners remain accountable for their own activities. The auditor assesses the plan. Management owns it, funds it, and accepts what risk remains.

4.15.1 Disruptions

Sections 4.15.1 and 4.15.2: IT continuity and disruptive events.

Not a strategy: Align technology recovery with approved business priorities and service levels, and integrate it with crisis management, emergency response and suppliers. Manual alternatives need capacity, controls, instructions and reconciliation. A statement that staff will use spreadsheets is not a tested strategy.

Consider facility loss, utility interruption, telecommunications failure, cyberattack, key supplier failure, data corruption, severe weather and workforce unavailability.

  • Facility loss: Building unavailable
  • Utilities and telecoms: Power or network gone
  • Cyberattack: And data corruption
  • Supplier failure: Key provider fails
  • Severe weather: Or workforce unavailability

Correlated Events: Assess correlated events that affect both primary and alternate arrangements. And focus on consequences and dependencies, as well as named scenarios, so the plan can adapt to unexpected causes.

Workforce disruption can leave facilities and servers intact while key activities cannot be staffed. Plan cross training, deputies, remote access, equipment, supplier resilience and sustainable workloads, because people can't work flat out for weeks. A crisis can also damage reputation through inaccurate information or poor handling, even when systems recover. So define authoritative communications, approved channels and spokespersons.

  • Staffing: Facilities intact, nobody to staff
  • Preparation: Cross-training, deputies, remote access
  • Reputation: Poor handling hurts after recovery
  • Authoritative voice: Approved channels and spokespersons

Flexible Decisions: Scenario planning should allow flexible decisions, rather than assume that every future event will match a predefined script. Large scale remote work can also increase identity, connectivity and support demands.

4.15.3 Process and policy

Sections 4.15.3 and 4.15.4: the planning process and business continuity policy.

Establish policy, scope, responsibilities and sponsorship. Analyze business impacts and risk. Evaluate and approve strategies. Develop coordinated plans. Train and exercise. And maintain and improve.

  • Policy and sponsorship: Scope and responsibilities
  • Analyze: Business impacts and risk
  • Approve strategies: Evaluate first
  • Develop plans: Coordinated
  • Exercise: Train and exercise
  • Maintain: And improve

Order Matters: The B I A identifies needs, the strategy selects how to meet them, and plans provide executable responsibilities and steps. Buying a site before understanding needs risks paying for the wrong capability.

The policy, endorsed at the top, states objectives, scope, accountability, risk expectations, planning and exercise requirements, maintenance responsibilities and reporting.

  • Objectives and scope: What it protects
  • Accountability: Who answers
  • Planning and exercise: Requirements
  • Reporting: To management

Paper Policy: Senior management should endorse the program and fund an appropriate capability. A policy document without owners or resources is not an operating continuity program.

4.15.5 Incidents and plan content

Sections 4.15.5 to 4.15.7: incident management, plan components, people and supplies.

Define assessment, escalation, crisis coordination and invocation authority. Name primary decision makers and deputies, and make thresholds and contact routes clear, so nobody wastes the first hour deciding who decides.

  • Assessment: Is this an incident?
  • Escalation: Thresholds and contact routes
  • Invocation authority: Named decision-makers
  • Deputies: If the primary is unreachable

Availability of people: Avoid relying on the person most likely to be unavailable. And protect life and safety, coordinating with emergency services as appropriate, before treating technical recovery as the only priority.

Plans need decision authority, covering who assesses, declares, invokes and authorizes return to normal. People and coordination. Business procedures with alternate methods and reconciliation. Resources and dependencies. Communications through approved channels. Recovery and resumption, including backlog handling and failback. And maintenance and evidence, such as exercises and version control.

  • Decision authority: Declare, invoke, return to normal
  • People and coordination: Call trees and shifts
  • Business procedures: Alternate methods, reconciliation
  • Resources: Facilities, suppliers, data
  • Communications: Approved channels
  • Recovery and resumption: Sequence, backlog, failback
  • Maintenance and evidence: Exercises, version control

Usable under disruption: Plans should be usable under disruption, with clear versions and accessible copies, including when ordinary systems and authentication are unavailable. Keep detailed sensitive credentials in appropriately protected recovery arrangements, rather than ordinary uncontrolled plan copies.

Supplies: Define crisis coordination, emergency response, damage assessment, business recovery, technology recovery, relocation and salvage responsibilities. Ensure roles don't conflict and substitutes are trained, so one person isn't assigned two jobs that happen at once. Supplies include devices, connectivity, payment methods, forms and access to vital records.

4.15.8 Insurance, testing, maintenance

Sections 4.15.8 to 4.15.10: insurance, plan testing, and maintenance.

Insurance Limit: Insurance can transfer specified financial consequences, subject to terms, exclusions, limits and conditions. It does not perform operational recovery. Continuity still needs people, resources, executable procedures and suitable technology.

Define objectives, scope, assumptions, scenario, participants, safety constraints and measurable success criteria. Exercise business processes and communications as well as technology. Increasing disruption doesn't automatically make an exercise better designed.

  • Objectives and scope: Plus assumptions
  • Scenario and participants: Safety constraints
  • Success criteria: Measurable
  • Business and technology: Both exercised

Failed Exercises: Document actual timelines, decisions and deviations, compare with objectives, and assign corrective actions. Retest significant weaknesses, so the fix is proven and not just assumed. A failed exercise can be valuable evidence for improvement. Declaring success by excluding the difficult dependencies conceals risk.

Update plans after material changes, incidents, exercises, organizational changes and supplier changes, as well as through scheduled reviews.

  • Material changes: Business, supplier, organizational
  • Incidents and exercises: Lessons applied
  • Scheduled reviews: Plus walkthroughs

Accuracy Trap: Walkthroughs can reveal stale contacts or missing steps, but controlled change integration and accountable updates also keep content current. Distribution and encryption protect access; they don't ensure accuracy. And don't weaken necessary resilience merely to make a plan look cheaper.

4.15.11 Auditing continuity

Assess sponsorship, B I A, strategy alignment, plan ownership, dependencies, contracts, resources, exercises and follow-up. Interview business and technical personnel to confirm they understand their roles. And compare the plan with actual infrastructure, staffing, suppliers and recovery evidence. A plan that describes last year's organization is an audit finding.

  • Sponsorship and BIA: Plus strategy alignment
  • Ownership and dependencies: Contracts and resources
  • Exercises and follow-up: Actions closed
  • Interviews: Do people know their roles?

Management owns the decision: Evaluate offsite storage access and protection, and inspect alternate processing commitments for capacity, availability during shared disasters, test rights and technical fit. Audit provides an assessment. Management owns invocation decisions, capability and acceptance of residual risk.

A mobile recovery site is transportable capability, often in a vehicle or container. Its usefulness depends on delivery time, site access, power, connectivity, compatible equipment and current data. A service bureau is a commercial provider of outsourced processing, potentially serving several customers. It is not exclusively a disaster recovery facility, so verify contracted compatible capacity, activation time and simultaneous demand assumptions.

  • Mobile site: Transportable capability
  • Delivery and access: Power, connectivity, equipment
  • Service bureau: Commercial outsourced processing
  • Contracted capacity: Not a general subscription

General Subscription: Equipment in transit doesn't satisfy an immediate service need, and a general subscription doesn't reserve adequate recovery capacity. We'll compare these alternatives in section 4.16.

Now the auditor perspective.

Real-World Example: The recovery application works, but no authorized staff can release urgent payments from the alternate location. Exercise business roles, access, verification and communications, as well as technical recovery. A technical success can still be a business failure. Manual processing needs realistic capacity, fraud controls, records and later reconciliation.

Paper Orders: During an outage, staff record urgent orders on paper. At service resumption, the same orders can be entered twice if the paper sequence isn't reconciled. Check authority to invoke the workaround, numbering, approvals, custody and backlog reconciliation, and who decides when to return to normal.

Practice questions

Test yourself with three exam-style questions. To recap. A B C P goes beyond technology, and a disaster recovery plan doesn't replace it. Define authority, people, deputies, access and supplies. Test the business, including manual workarounds through reconciliation. And keep the plan current, because an approval date isn't evidence.

  • Beyond technology: DR doesn't replace the BCP
  • Authority and people: Deputies, access, supplies
  • Test the business: And its manual workarounds
  • Keep it current: Not just approved

Practice questions

1. An enterprise presents its IT disaster recovery plan as its business continuity plan. What should the IS auditor conclude? Answer: Technology is covered, but people, premises and suppliers are not. Option B assumes technology is the only dependency. Option C treats buying an alternate site as the plan, but a site isn't a plan. And option D confuses approval with coverage. A signature doesn't add people, premises and suppliers.

2. An alternate site's recovery application works, yet no authorized staff can release urgent payments. What does this MOST likely indicate? Answer: The exercise covered technology but not business roles and access. Option A, insurance, covers financial consequences but doesn't supply people or access. Option B blames the recovery time objective, but the application did recover. And option D, unencrypted media, is a different control and doesn't explain the missing authority.

3. Which is the BEST indicator that a business continuity plan remains reliable? Answer: Changes trigger updates, and exercise findings are tracked to retest. Option A, a recent approval date, doesn't show that resources and assumptions remain valid. Option B, encryption and distribution, protect access but not accuracy. And option C, a walkthrough alone, can reveal stale contacts but doesn't integrate change or close exercise findings.
